\section{The Shared Variant-Byte Namespace}
\label{sec:shared-namespace}

The single most important piece of shared infrastructure in \tessera{} is not a library --- it is a one-byte namespace. The variant byte at offset 6 of every wire format names one of the three projects (or a permanent-reserved sentinel). Cross-project decoders reject every byte they do not own.

\subsection{Allocation as of v1.x}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Byte} & \textbf{Owner} & \textbf{Meaning} & \textbf{Status} \\
\midrule
\code{0x00}     & ---        & Permanently reserved (never assign) & MUST reject \\
\code{0x01}     & \tesseraid{}    & Standard generator                    & assigned v1.0 \\
\code{0x02}     & \tesseraid{}    & HLC generator                         & assigned v1.0 \\
\code{0x03}     & \tesserahash{}  & SHAKE-256 / 64-byte tail              & assigned v1.0 \\
\code{0x04}     & \tesseraseal{} & Encryption envelope (suite byte at offset 7) & assigned v1.0 \\
\code{0x05}--\code{0xFE} & --- & Reserved-future                  & MUST reject \\
\code{0xFF}     & ---        & Permanently reserved (never assign) & MUST reject \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The reservation of \code{0x00} and \code{0xFF} is defensive: zero-filled or all-ones buffers cannot pass validation as any project's wire form. The reserved-future range \code{0x05}--\code{0xFE} is available for additional projects under \tessera{}; each new allocation requires a normative whitepaper update and conformance-vector additions to every existing project (to verify cross-rejection).

\subsection{The cross-rejection contract}

Every project's decoder MUST reject every variant byte it does not own:

\begin{itemize}
\item \tesseraid{} decoders accept \code{0x01} and \code{0x02} only. Bytes \code{0x03} (TesseraHash), \code{0x04} (TesseraSeal), and every reserved-future byte produce \texttt{UnknownVariant} / \texttt{TESSERA\_ID\_E\_UNKNOWN\_VARIANT}.
\item \tesserahash{} decoders accept \code{0x03} only. Bytes \code{0x01}, \code{0x02}, \code{0x04}, and reserved-future bytes produce \texttt{TESSERA\_HASH\_E\_UNKNOWN\_VAR}.
\item \tesseraseal{} decoders accept \code{0x04} only. Bytes \code{0x01}, \code{0x02}, \code{0x03}, and reserved-future bytes produce \texttt{UnknownVariant} / \texttt{TESSERA\_SEAL\_E\_UNKNOWN\_VARIANT}.
\end{itemize}

The contract applies at every decode surface, not just the binary one: the Base64URL and Crockford text decoders MUST validate the variant byte at decode time, exactly as the binary decoders do. As of 2026-06-10 all eight language ports of both text-encoded projects (\tesseraid{}, \tesserahash{}) enforce this, and string-form unknown-variant records in the \texttt{invalid.json} KAT files pin it.

The contract is gated by \texttt{invalid.json} KAT files in every project's \texttt{conformance/vectors/} directory. A decoder that accepts a foreign variant byte fails the cross-language harness; the bug is in the decoder, not in the harness.

\subsection{Why one namespace and not three}

A naive design would let each project define its own variant byte in its own one-byte field. We chose a single shared namespace for three reasons:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Confusion attacks.} If a decoder sees the first nine bytes of a wire form and the four bytes ``timestamp + variant + counter'' happen to look valid by every project's local rules, the decoder must decide which project to dispatch to. With a single namespace, the variant byte is the dispatcher; there is no ambiguity.
\item \textbf{Substitution attacks.} An attacker who knows a system accepts \tesseraid{}s as primary keys and \tesserahash{}es as content keys could substitute one for the other if the variant bytes were locally-scoped. With cross-rejection enforced by the namespace, the substitution fails at the decoder.
\item \textbf{Future allocation discipline.} A single, externally-visible byte allocation table makes it impossible to retroactively claim ``oh, our project always meant \code{0x01} to be \emph{this}'' --- the table is normative across projects, version-controlled, and tested.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The 9-byte shared header}

The first 9 bytes of every project's wire format are layout-compatible: \code{TS(6) || VARIANT(1) || COUNTER(2)}, big-endian.

\begin{itemize}
\item \code{TS(6)}: 48-bit Unix millisecond timestamp. Every project uses the same constant \texttt{MIN\_TIMESTAMP\_MS = 1\_700\_000\_000\_000} as a sanity-check lower bound.
\item \code{VARIANT(1)}: the cross-project namespace byte.
\item \code{COUNTER(2)}: 16-bit big-endian monotonic counter within a millisecond.
\end{itemize}

The C header at \texttt{common/c/tessera\_header.h} records those offsets; today it is included by \tesseraseal{}'s C port, while the \tesseraid{} and \tesserahash{} C ports define the same offsets locally (deliberate duplication, per the \texttt{common/} policy of lifting code only once sharing is real --- the cross-language harness keeps the layouts identical). Code that just wants to read the timestamp from any \tessera{} wire form parses the first 6 bytes without knowing or caring which project produced it. Code that wants to dispatch on project reads byte 6.

The 9-byte boundary is where the projects diverge: \tesseraid{} writes a 32-byte random tail, \tesserahash{} writes a 64-byte SHAKE-256 tail, and \tesseraseal{} writes a 7-byte body-header continuation (suite + KEM\_CT\_LEN + AD\_LEN) followed by a variable body.

\subsection{Encoding overlap}

\tesseraid{} and \tesserahash{} both encode to Base64URL (RFC 4648 §5) and Crockford Base32. The encoder code paths are identical across the two projects in every language (with different alphabet sizes per project). \tesseraseal{} ships binary-only on the wire: envelope sizes vary, the variable-length text-encoder is more involved than the fixed-length ones, and most callers wrap envelopes with standard Base64URL at the transport layer if they need text.

TesseraID and TesseraHash both guard the trailing padding bits on decode, but the pad widths differ per project because the wire sizes differ. \tesseraid{} (41 bytes = 328 bits): the 55-character Base64URL form ends with a character carrying 4 bits of value plus 2 zero pad bits, and the 66-character Crockford form ends with 3 bits of value plus 2 zero pad bits --- in both encodings the final character's low 2 bits MUST be zero. \tesserahash{} (73 bytes = 584 bits): the 98-character Base64URL form ends with 2 bits of value plus 4 zero pad bits (the final character's low 4 bits MUST be zero, so it is always one of \code{A}/\code{Q}/\code{g}/\code{w}), and the 117-character Crockford form ends with 4 bits of value plus 1 zero pad bit (the final character's low bit MUST be zero). These guards prevent multiple distinct text strings from decoding to the same binary, which would violate canonical round-trip.
