\section{Canonical Cross-Project Patterns}
\label{sec:patterns}

The three projects are designed to compose. This section documents the canonical patterns and the reasoning behind each.

\subsection{An end-to-end walkthrough}

Before the individual patterns, here is the whole chain at once --- one training shard moving through a cluster, touching all three formats, with no central coordinator anywhere in the path:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Identity (\tesseraid{}).} The producing node mints a \tesseraid{} for the shard: 41 bytes, generated locally, unique without a coordinator, and time-sortable so the shard lands in order in an append-mostly store.
\item \textbf{Integrity (\tesserahash{}).} It computes a \tesserahash{} over the shard's bytes. Two nodes that independently hold the same shard derive the same content tail, so duplicates across the corpus collapse to one, and any consumer can verify that the bytes it received are the bytes that were committed --- no key, no manifest, no coordinator.
\item \textbf{Confidentiality (\tesseraseal{}).} It seals the shard for the consumer's ML-KEM-1024 public key, binding the \tesseraid{} as the AEAD associated data (Pattern~1 below). The envelope is now post-quantum-confidential \emph{and} cryptographically pinned to that record: an attacker who re-points it at a different \tesseraid{} gets a generic \texttt{DecryptionError}, nothing more.
\item \textbf{Content-addressed storage.} The cluster stores the envelope at a key derived from the \tesserahash{} of the \emph{ciphertext} (Pattern~4 below), so identical sealed objects deduplicate at the storage layer.
\item \textbf{Consumption.} A consumer node fetches the envelope, opens it with its private key, and re-derives the \tesserahash{} of the recovered plaintext to confirm it received exactly the committed content. Identity, integrity, and confidentiality were each provided by exactly one format, and the three composed without a single round-trip to a coordinator.
\end{enumerate}

That is the unifying value: one record, three orthogonal guarantees, one shared wire discipline. The patterns below formalize each link in this chain and name the threat each one closes.

\subsection{Pattern 1: \tesseraid{}-as-AD (record-level confidentiality binding)}

\textbf{Use when:} a record has a stable \tesseraid{} identifier and the record's content needs confidentiality and integrity tied to that identifier.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Pseudocode]
record_id  = tessera_id.new_hlc()                              # 41 bytes, variant 0x02
envelope   = tessera_seal.encrypt(plaintext, recipient_pub,
                             associated_data=record_id)  # bind TesseraID as AEAD AD
# ... after delivery ...
plaintext  = tessera_seal.decrypt(envelope, recipient_priv,
                             expected_ad=record_id)      # MUST match
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{What it defends:} an attacker who reroutes the envelope under a different \tesseraid{} (e.g., to make a leaked envelope appear to belong to a different record) gets AEAD-AD mismatch on decrypt, which produces \tesseraseal{}'s generic \texttt{DecryptionError}. The envelope is cryptographically bound to the identifier it was issued for.

\textbf{Cost:} zero additional cryptography. The AEAD AD path is free in every AEAD construction. The TesseraID is 41 bytes; the AEAD AD field has a 64\,KiB cap (\tesseraseal{} \code{AD\_LEN $\leq 65{,}536$}).

\textbf{Test coverage:} \texttt{tessera-seal/conformance/vectors/ad\_binding.json} exercises positive (matching AD decrypts) and negative (any AD bit-flip $\to$ \texttt{DECRYPT\_FAILED}) cases.

This is the most-used cross-project pattern, named the ``canonical pattern'' in \tesseraseal{}'s normative whitepaper.

\subsection{Pattern 2: \tesserahash{}-as-AD (content-level confidentiality binding)}

\textbf{Use when:} the encrypted payload must be bound to specific content bytes, not just ``some content this recipient can decrypt.''

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Pseudocode]
content_hash = tessera_hash.hash_only(plaintext)               # 64 bytes
envelope     = tessera_seal.encrypt(plaintext, recipient_pub,
                               associated_data=content_hash)
# ... after delivery ...
plaintext    = tessera_seal.decrypt(envelope, recipient_priv,
                               expected_ad=content_hash)
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{What it defends:} an attacker who substitutes the envelope with one carrying different content (but the same recipient key and structurally-valid wire form) fails decryption because the AEAD AD they would supply does not match the embedded content. The pattern composes with UtC commitment (\tesseraseal{}'s 32-byte commitment tag, \citep{bellare2022commitment}): UtC closes multi-recipient key confusion; \tesserahash{}-as-AD closes content substitution. The two bindings are independent.

\textbf{Variant:} instead of passing just the 64-byte SHAKE-256 tail, pass the full 73-byte \tesserahash{} as AD. This carries provenance (the \tesserahash{}'s timestamp and counter) alongside the content commitment. The choice depends on whether the AD-binding consumer needs the provenance metadata.

\subsection{Pattern 3: \tesseraid{} + \tesserahash{} (identifier + content commitment)}

\textbf{Use when:} a record-keeping system tracks both record identity (which mutates rarely) and content (which mutates often).

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Pseudocode]
record_id    = tessera_id.new_hlc()           # immutable for the record's lifetime
content_hash = tessera_hash.hash_only(...)    # rolls on every content update
# Persist {record_id, content_hash, body} as the canonical record view.
# Subscribers verify both: record_id pins identity, content_hash pins content.
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{What it defends:} content-substitution attacks at the storage layer. An attacker who modifies a record's body without rolling the \tesserahash{} produces inconsistent state that storage-level integrity checks detect. Independent of any cryptographic envelope.

\subsection{Pattern 4: \tesserahash{} of a \tesseraseal{} envelope (content-addressed ciphertext)}

\textbf{Use when:} an object-storage system indexes by ciphertext rather than plaintext.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Pseudocode]
envelope     = tessera_seal.encrypt(plaintext, recipient_pub)
storage_key  = tessera_hash.hash_only(envelope)               # 64-byte SHAKE-256
# Store at key=storage_key; lookup by key=tessera_hash.hash_only(known_envelope_bytes).
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{What it defends:} accidental duplication of identical ciphertexts under different storage keys. \tesseraseal{} envelopes are deterministic in their inputs (plaintext, recipient key, AD, sender randomness): two envelopes with the same input randomness are byte-identical, so \tesserahash{}-keying coalesces them. With different sender randomness, two envelopes for the same plaintext differ, so deduplication does not collapse them --- this is the right behavior for a system that wants per-encryption auditability.

\subsection{Pattern 5: \tesseraid{}-tagged \tesserahash{} (provenance-tagged content commitment)}

\textbf{Use when:} content has multiple producers and the audit log needs to record ``producer X committed to content Y at time T.''

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Pseudocode]
record_id = tessera_id.new_hlc()              # who is committing
content_h = tessera_hash.hash_only(content)   # what they are committing to
audit_entry = sign(record_id || content_h, producer_signing_key)
# Persist audit_entry. Note: TesseraSeal is not involved if the content is public;
# wrap with TesseraSeal only when confidentiality is also required.
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{Note:} \tessera{} does not (yet) ship a signature primitive. The signing step is performed with an external scheme: ML-DSA \citep{fips204} for FIPS-aligned PQ signing, Ed25519 \citep{rfc8032} for classical, or a hybrid via PASETO \citep{paseto-v4}. A future \texttt{pqsign}-style sibling project is outside v1.x scope.

\subsection{Anti-patterns}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Using \tesseraid{} as the AEAD key.} \tesseraid{} is a public identifier, not a key. Anyone who has seen the \tesseraid{} can forge an envelope under it. Use a proper key from a plugin (\tesseraseal{}'s \texttt{tessera-seal-plugin-file} or an HSM-backed plugin).
\item \textbf{Using \tesserahash{} as a password.} \tesserahash{}'s tail is content-deterministic and keyless. An attacker who can guess the content recomputes the tail. Use Argon2id \citep{rfc9106} or scrypt for password derivation; \tesserahash{}'s threat model explicitly excludes secrecy of low-entropy content.
\item \textbf{Truncating to fewer bytes than the wire format prescribes.} \tesseraid{} is exactly 41 bytes; \tesserahash{} is exactly 73 bytes; \tesseraseal{}'s header is exactly 16 bytes. ``Optimizing'' by dropping fields breaks the wire contract and the cross-language harness.
\item \textbf{Mixing variant bytes within an artifact.} A 41-byte buffer whose variant byte is \code{0x03} is not a \tesseraid{}; it is a malformed input that the \tesseraid{} decoder rejects (and the \tesserahash{} decoder also rejects, because the length is wrong). Don't try to ``upgrade'' a \tesseraid{} into a \tesserahash{} by editing one byte.
\end{itemize}
