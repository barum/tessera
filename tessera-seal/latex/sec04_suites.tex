\section{Algorithm Suites}
\label{sec:suites}

The \code{SUITE} byte at offset 7 selects a complete algorithm bundle. v1.x allocates three:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}clllll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Suite} & \textbf{KEM(s)} & \textbf{AEAD} & \textbf{KDF/MAC} & \textbf{Status} & \textbf{Notes} \\
\midrule
\code{0x01} & ML-KEM-1024 & AES-256-GCM + UtC commit (32 B) & HKDF-SHA3-256 & \textbf{mandatory} & v1 default, pure PQ \\
\code{0x02} & ML-KEM-1024 & ChaCha20-Poly1305 + UtC commit (32 B) & HKDF-SHA3-256 & optional & software-only, pure PQ \\
\code{0x06} & X25519 + ML-KEM-1024 (\cref{sec:hybrid}) & AES-256-GCM + UtC commit (32 B) & HKDF-SHA3-256 & optional & hybrid, v1.2 (PQCK-00021) \\
\code{0x00}, \code{0x03}--\code{0x05}, \code{0x07}--\code{0xFF} & --- reject --- & --- & --- & --- & reserved-future \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The wire is unambiguous about which guarantee a given envelope makes: suites \code{0x01}/\code{0x02} commit to ``post-quantum confidentiality, no classical fallback''; suite \code{0x06} commits to ``post-quantum \emph{or} classical confidentiality, the attacker must break both.'' Suite \code{0x06} is a \emph{separate suite-byte allocation} (\cref{sec:hybrid}); it is never an in-place modification of \code{0x01}/\code{0x02}. Callers select by passing the suite byte explicitly to \code{encrypt}; \code{decrypt} dispatches by the suite byte in the envelope header.

\textbf{Why ML-KEM-1024 only, not ML-KEM-768?} The core promise (\code{0x01}/\code{0x02}) has no hybrid safety net (see \cref{sec:threat-model}). We buy the largest NIST-defined PQ margin (level 5, $\sim$AES-256-equivalent classical-search) at the cost of a 1568 B KEM ciphertext (vs 1088 B for ML-KEM-768). For envelope use this $\sim$500 B difference is irrelevant; the security margin is not. Suite \code{0x06} keeps ML-KEM-1024 (not 768) for the same reason: even with X25519 as a backup, the PQ half of the hybrid should not be downgraded.

\textbf{Why AES-256-GCM + UtC, not AEGIS or GCM-SIV?} AEGIS-128L is 2--3$\times$ faster on modern x86 and ARM \citep{rfc9678} but its RFC finalized only September 2025; library support is immature. AES-256-GCM has decade-scale review, AES-NI/VAES acceleration to $\sim$10 GB/s \citep{intelisa,ebacs}, and a settled commitment construction in UtC \citep{bellare2022commitment}. GCM-SIV duplicates the misuse-resistance property that the deterministic-nonce-from-KDF already provides structurally (\cref{sec:kdf-nonce}). AEGIS-128L was evaluated for v1.2 (a hypothetical suite \code{0x03}) in PQCK-00020 and \emph{deferred}: as of the v1.2 evaluation (2026-05-21), AEGIS implementations were not yet polyglot-ready across Python, Go, Java, and OpenSSL. The byte remains reserved-future; revisit when verified implementations land across libcrux, OpenSSL, and the Go stdlib.

\textbf{Why HKDF-SHA3-256?} SHAKE/Keccak is already vendored under \code{common/c/keccak.\{h,c\}} for \pqhash{}; HKDF-SHA3 reuses that primitive and aligns with FIPS 203's internal use of Keccak. SHA-256 with SHA-NI is faster but creates two hash families to audit and certify.

\textbf{No agility within a suite.} A suite byte \emph{is} a version: changing any algorithm requires a new suite byte. There is no negotiation. This matches \pqid{}'s and \pqhash{}'s immutable-variant-byte discipline and avoids the downgrade and cross-protocol attack surface of TLS-style negotiation (cf.~Terrapin \citep{terrapin}).
